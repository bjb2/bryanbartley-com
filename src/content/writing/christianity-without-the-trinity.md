I want to argue for something most theologians will regard as settled: that a person can hold the whole of the apostolic faith, worship Jesus as Lord, and expect salvation through him, without believing that God is three persons in one essence. The New Testament, read in the idiom its authors actually spoke, stands with such a person rather than with the Nicene who would exclude him. The case has two parts. The first is historical and exegetical. The confession of the earliest churches was that there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus, whom this God sent, raised from the dead and enthroned at his right hand; and that confession turns out to be complete, needing nothing from the fourth century to make it saving. The second part is constructive. I offer an account, which for want of a better name I call **Agent Christology**, on which the Word of John's Prologue is God's own self-communicating act rather than a second subject, on which that act became a human life in Jesus of Nazareth, and on which the lordship the risen Jesus now exercises was given to him by God and is exercised toward God's glory.

Much of what follows turns on a question that is rarely put directly. **If the first Christians thought about Jesus the way Nicaea says they did, what should their letters and gospels look like?** We should expect him to be called God in the creeds, to receive the kind of cult Israel reserved for YHWH, to stand level with the Father once exalted, and we should expect the Spirit to be addressed as the third of three. None of this appears. What appears instead is a lordship that God confers, a worship that passes through Jesus to the Father, a risen Christ who still speaks of "my God," and a Spirit who in thirteen Pauline greetings is never once named as a source of grace. These are the marks of an agent, and Second Temple Judaism had a well-developed way of thinking about God's agents which the New Testament writers inherited and used. I set this out through the texts that matter most, place it in its Jewish setting, show that the church's own teachers held something like it for three centuries, and then take the Nicene objections one at a time, Hurtado and Bauckham included.

## 1. Method and the standard of necessity

It matters to be clear about what is being denied. The Nicene churches treat assent to the Nicene Creed as the line between those who are inside the church and those who are not. The Athanasian Creed opens with *quicumque vult salvus esse*, Catholic and Orthodox canon law ties the validity of baptism to the Trinitarian formula, and when the World Council of Churches revised its basis of membership in 1961 it did so in order to make the Trinity explicit. So **the claim I am contesting is not "God is triune" but "one must believe God is triune in order to be a Christian,"** and that second claim is a good deal more exposed than the first. Whoever adds a condition to the apostolic faith owes the rest of us an account of where the apostles imposed it. If no such place can be found, the condition is an addition; and an addition that would have excluded Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian and Origen is measuring something other than apostolic Christianity.

I try to meet that burden on the Nicene tradition's terms:

1. I read the New Testament in its first-century Jewish idiom, taking *theos*, *kyrios*, *logos*, *monogenēs* and "Son of God" in the senses their authors had available. This is the method the early-high-Christology school claims for itself, and a good part of my argument consists in holding that school to it.
2. I treat the history of doctrine as evidence rather than as background. If the men who gave us the canon held views the councils later anathematized, then either they were not Christians or the councils were not describing what it takes to be one. The Nicene tradition cannot accept the first option and, so far as I can see, has never squarely faced the second.
3. I take coherence to be a test the doctrine has to pass rather than a mystery it is allowed to invoke. After forty years of close analytic work the doctrine's defenders cannot agree on what it asserts, and each school regards the others' version as heresy. A proposition whose content is in that condition is a strange candidate for the object of saving belief.

The standard of proof is the ordinary one in historical theology. I do not need to show that a Nicene reading of any particular verse is impossible; the tradition has had sixteen centuries to make its readings possible. What I hope to show is that the pattern of the whole runs the other way, so that the Nicene reading survives only by explaining away its counter-evidence one verse at a time, which is the posture of a theory that is no longer offering the more natural reading. Agent Christology, I should add, is a construction and not a direct retrieval of earlier traditions. It is not modalism, Arianism, Socinianism or Nestorianism, and §2 shows how it differs from each.

## 2. Agent Christology stated

Let me state my position plainly, since a good deal of the Nicene case consists in attacking views I do not hold.

1. There is one divine subject, the God of Israel, whom Jesus and the apostles call the Father. When the New Testament says *theos* it ordinarily means this person; the word occurs some 1,300 times, and apart from references to false gods and a handful of disputed cases the Father is its referent. I do not think this is a reductive observation about the text so much as the plain shape of it.
2. God's Word and God's Wisdom are God's own activity, personified. The Word that goes out from God's mouth and does not return empty (Isa 55:11), the Wisdom who was beside him as a master workman at creation (Prov 8:30), the Spirit brooding over the deep (Gen 1:2), are Israel's ways of speaking about God doing things. Bauckham himself sets Word, Wisdom and Spirit in a category of their own, distinct from angels and exalted patriarchs, as personifications "included within the divine identity." I accept the category and draw what seems to me the obvious inference from it, which is that a personification of God's activity is not a second person.
3. In Jesus of Nazareth that activity became a human life. John does not say that a second divine person assumed a human nature; he says the Word became flesh. I take that to mean that **God's self-expression took the form of a complete human being**, with a human mind, a human will and a human relation to God. Whatever pre-existence Scripture attributes to Christ is the pre-existence of God's Word and purpose, which is eternal, and out of which Jesus speaks. The difference between Jesus and a prophet is one of kind, not degree. A prophet exists first and is then visited: the word of the LORD *comes to* Jeremiah (Jer 1:4). Jesus does not exist first. His human life is brought into being by God's Spirit and power from conception (Luke 1:35), so there is no moment at which he is a man to whom the Word later comes. The relation is the one a person has to his own speech, which is not a second person beside him but the person himself in expression; when that expression is a human life, the life is God's self-utterance without ceasing to be a human life with its own mind and will. The analogy has limits, as every analogy here does, but it puts the union in the right place: not in a second divine subject who adopts a human nature, and not in a human subject whom God afterwards uses, but in a human existence whose whole content, from its origin, is God's own Word. Geoffrey Lampe (*God as Spirit*, 1977) and D. M. Baillie (*God Was in Christ*, 1948) attempted versions of this and were criticised for reducing it to inspiration. The answer to that criticism is the conception, which no theory of inspiration includes.
4. Jesus is God's plenipotentiary, his ***shaliach***. The maxim preserved in *b. Qiddushin* 41b, "a man's agent is as himself," gives us the native Jewish category for the Johannine sending formulae. Peder Borgen showed the fit in 1968 and E. L. Allen had seen it fifteen years earlier. The one sent carries the sender's full authority, is honoured as the sender is honoured, acts in the sender's name, and remains subordinate to him. Once that is in view, the Johannine paradoxes stop being paradoxes. "I and the Father are one" and "the Father is greater than I," "whoever has seen me has seen the Father" and "the Son can do nothing of himself," are simply the two sides of agency.
5. God raised Jesus and made him Lord of everything. Luke's Peter says God *made* him Lord and Messiah (Acts 2:36); Paul's hymn says God *gave* him the name above every name (Phil 2:9); Matthew's Jesus says all authority *has been given* to him (28:18). The lordship is conferred, which is why its glory returns to the Father (Phil 2:11) and why at the end it is handed back (1 Cor 15:24–28).
6. The Spirit is God's presence and power. No one in the New Testament prays to the Spirit, worships the Spirit, or receives grace "from" the Spirit in a greeting. The Spirit is the mode in which God and the risen Christ are present to the church, not a third party standing beside them.

| Arianism | The Son is a pre-existent creature, "there was when he was not" | Nothing created pre-exists; what pre-exists is God's own Word, uncreated because it is God's |

| Adoptionism | Jesus became Son at baptism or resurrection | Jesus is Son from conception (Luke 1:35); the resurrection *vindicates* and *exalts* rather than adopts |
| Nestorianism (as condemned) | Two persons, divine and human, in moral conjunction | There is no second divine *person* to conjoin; the union is of God's act and a human life |

The nearest historical relatives are the Antiochene insistence on the integral humanity of Christ, Paul of Samosata as far as he can be reconstructed from the Antioch fragments, and among moderns the agency readings of Dunn and Kirk. I am not trying to revive them, I am trying to say what the New Testament writers were doing, in the categories they had, and then to see whether a Christian can stop there.

## 3. Exegetical foundations

What strikes me most about the New Testament's confessional formulae, once one stops reading them through the creeds, is how consistently binitary they are in shape and how monotheistic in reference. There are six distinct bodies of evidence that I use to support this.

**3.1 The Pauline Shema (1 Cor 8:6)**

Paul's "one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist" is the earliest Christian creed we have, and no one doubts that it reworks Deuteronomy 6:4. Bauckham and Wright read it as Paul putting Jesus *inside* the Shema. I would rather look at what Paul actually did with the two divine titles he found there. He gave *theos* to the Father and *kyrios* to Jesus, and he distributed the prepositions unevenly: all things are *from* (*ek*) the Father and *through* (*dia*) the Lord. That is the grammar of a source and his agent, and Paul has told us two verses earlier whom he means by the source: "there is no God but one" (8:4). Dunn's judgment seems to me exactly right, that Paul here assigns Jesus YHWH's *role* as Lord without identifying him with YHWH's person; in Dunn's own formulation, "if Jesus is God he is not YHWH." ” C. A. Wanamaker reached the same conclusion from the agency material itself, arguing in “Christ as Divine Agent in Paul” (1986) that Paul’s Christology is built on the Jewish pattern of God acting through a commissioned agent. It is also worth saying what the verse does not contain. There is no Spirit in it. A creed that supposedly enshrines the Trinity and names two of its persons is better explained as a creed enshrining something else.

**3.2 The grammar of the Spirit**

If the Spirit were a third divine person, one would expect to find him in the places a person belongs. However, we find little support in the texts.

- All thirteen Pauline letters open with a grace greeting, and in eleven of them the grace comes from God the Father and Christ (Colossians, in the better manuscripts, names only "God our Father"; 1 Thessalonians names no source). The Spirit is never a source of greeting. Revelation 1:4 does include "the seven spirits before his throne" in its greeting, but as a plural throne-image rather than a named person, and the book's worship (5:13) is still offered to the throne and the Lamb alone.
- No New Testament prayer is addressed to the Spirit. Jesus instructs prayer to the Father (Matt 6:9; John 16:23).
- No doxology is addressed to the Spirit. Revelation's heavenly liturgy praises "the one seated on the throne and the Lamb" (5:13); the Spirit is "seven spirits before the throne" (1:4), a plural symbol.
- 1 Cor 12:4–6 and 2 Cor 13:14 are triadic, but they are also asymmetric: "the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Spirit" names two persons and one bond.

Nicene theology explains all this by the Spirit's "self-effacement," the Spirit being the person who points away from himself. The hypothesis is rarely argued for independently of the need for it, and it is unfalsifiable by construction, since every further absence confirms it. It will be said in reply that the Spirit has personal predicates: he grieves (Eph 4:30), speaks (Acts 13:2), intercedes (Rom 8:26), and distributes gifts "as he wills" (1 Cor 12:11). He does, and so does Wisdom, who cries aloud in the streets and prepares a banquet (Prov 1:20; 9:1–5), and so does the Word, which "runs swiftly" and "heals" (Ps 147:15; 107:20), and so does the Spirit of the Hebrew Bible, who is "grieved" at Isaiah 63:10 by a people who had no concept of a second or third divine person. Personal verbs are the native idiom for God's activity in Jewish writing. What that idiom never does is turn around and address the activity as a "you," and the New Testament, having every opportunity, never does either. Between a hypothesis that explains the verbs and the silence together and one that explains the verbs and must invent a reason for the silence, the first seems to me the better explanation; and since positing a third divine person is the heavier claim, the burden lies with the party making it.

**3.3 Jesus has a God, after Easter**

The risen Jesus tells Mary, "I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God" (John 20:17). The glorified Christ of the Apocalypse says "my God" four times in a single verse (Rev 3:12) and once more at 3:2. A characteristic Pauline way of naming God is "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 15:6; 2 Cor 1:3; 11:31; and, in the disputed letters, Eph 1:3; Col 1:3), and Ephesians 1:17 separates the two titles altogether: "the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory." The usual reply is that the human nature is speaking. But the Chalcedonian tradition, following Cyril, holds that actions and sayings belong to the person, not to a nature. (Leo's Tome, which Chalcedon received, can be read the other way, since it says each form "does what is proper to it"; but that reading only moves the problem, because a nature that says "my God" from heaven is then a speaking subject in its own right.) If the person who says "my God" from heaven is the second person of the Trinity, then the second person of the Trinity has a God, and that is all I am claiming.

**3.4 Permanent subordination (1 Cor 15:24–28)**

Paul’s account of the end of all things has Christ hand the kingdom over to God the Father, and then, he says, “the Son himself will also be subjected to him who put all things in subjection under him, that God may be all in all.” The scene comes after the incarnate economy has run its course. The verb, hypotassō, is the one Paul has just used three times in verse 27 for the subjection of “all things” to Christ, and he now uses it, in the form hypotagēsetai, of Christ himself. When Marcellus of Ancyra read the passage at face value, as teaching that the Word’s distinct reign would one day end, the church answered him with the creedal clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end,” and the Nicene tradition has had to argue ever since that “subjected” here means something other than subjected. Taken plainly, the verse describes an agent who finishes his commission and returns authority to the one who gave it.

**3.5 The Johannine Logos**

John says the Word was *with* God, *pros ton theon*, and that the Word was *theos*, anarthrous and in the predicate position before the verb. He then tells us what the Word's being *theos* comes to: "No one has ever seen God; the unique one, who is in the bosom of the Father, has *exegeted* him" (1:18). The earliest manuscripts (P66, P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) read *monogenēs theos* here, "the unique God," rather than "the unique Son," and I take that reading. It fits the Prologue's frame exactly: the Word that is *theos* in 1:1 is *theos* again in 1:18, and in both places the predicate says what the Word is as God's self-exegesis, not that a second subject stands beside him. The Word is God's exposition of himself. Philo, writing in the same idiom a generation earlier, noticed that Genesis 31:13 uses *theos* without the article and took it to name the Logos (*On Dreams* 1.229–230); he could call the Logos "the second God" and "God's firstborn" and remain a monotheist, because for him the Logos was God's reason at work in the world and not a rival to him. John belongs to that semantic world, and the Prologue's climax belongs there too. It does not say that the second person took flesh. It says the Word became flesh and we saw his glory, "glory as of the unique one from the Father" (1:14), which is to say the Father's own glory, exegeted in a human life.

A word about *monogenēs*, since so much hangs on it. The term means "only one of its kind," not "only-begotten"; Hebrews 11:17 calls Isaac Abraham's *monogenēs* although Ishmael was the elder, because what the author has in mind is Isaac's uniqueness, not his generation. The doctrine of eternal generation leans on the "begotten" reading, which the Greek will not bear, and on Psalm 2:7, "today I have begotten you," which Acts 13:33 takes as a prophecy of the resurrection.

**3.6 Jesus' own definition of eternal life**

In the prayer of John 17 Jesus says, "This is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent." I do not know how to improve on that as a summary of my position. The Father alone is "the only true God"; Jesus is identified by the fact that he was sent. The Fourth Gospel put these words in Jesus' mouth as its own account of what saving knowledge is, and it did so without any apparent sense that something was missing.

## 4. The Second Temple context: divine agency was a native category

The objection I meet most often is that Jewish monotheism had no room for a worshipped, enthroned, name-bearing figure who was not fully God, so the first Christians, in treating Jesus that way, must already have been thinking in something like Nicene terms. The Second Temple evidence says otherwise, and it is worth seeing why in detail. The objection also assumes that “monotheism” had sharper boundaries than ancient Judaism gave it; Peter Hayman argued in 1991 that the word misdescribes Jewish belief of the period.

Begin with the angel of Exodus 23, who bears God's name ("my name is in him"), speaks as God, and must be obeyed as God is obeyed. Philo's Logos is "the firstborn," "the second God" (*Questions on Genesis* 2.62), the instrument through which the world was made, the high priest, the one through whom God is seen; and Philo would have been astonished to be told he was not a monotheist. The Son of Man of the Enochic *Similitudes* was named before creation, sits on the throne of glory, is worshipped by "all who dwell on the earth" (1 Enoch 48:5), and in chapter 71 turns out to be the human Enoch. Ezekiel the Tragedian puts Moses on God's throne with God's sceptre in his hand while the stars bow to him. 11QMelchizedek reads Psalm 82's "*elohim* stands in the divine council" of Melchizedek and calls him *elohim*. In the *Life of Adam and Eve* Michael, on God's command, summons the angels to worship Adam as God's image. These are not obscure texts. Hurtado catalogued most of them in *One God, One Lord* in 1988, before deciding that Christ-devotion went beyond them, and the question I want to press is whether it did.

Alan Segal's *Two Powers in Heaven* (1977) is still the foundational study here. Segal showed that the rabbinic heresy of "two powers" was a reaction against a pattern of complementary divine agency that already existed in Jewish reading of Daniel 7 (two thrones, the Ancient of Days and one like a son of man), of Exodus 15:3 and of Exodus 23:20–23, and he dated the rabbinic condemnation to the second century, which means the pattern was alive and licit in the first. Daniel Boyarin has since pushed the argument further, in "Two Powers in Heaven; or, The Making of a Heresy" (2004) and *The Jewish Gospels* (2012): on his reading the enthroned Son of Man was a figure many Jews were expecting, and the Jesus of the Gospels steps into a role already prepared. Peter Schäfer (*Two Gods in Heaven*, 2020) thinks Boyarin overreads Daniel, but even Schäfer concedes the breadth of the binitarian material in Enoch, 4 Ezra and the Aramaic Apocryphon. One need not follow Boyarin all the way to see what this does to the Nicene objection. A first-century Jew could seat a second figure on God's throne, give him God's name and bow before him, and not thereby be asserting that the figure was the one God.

James McGrath, in *The Only True God* (2009), proposed a test for where the boundary of first-century monotheism actually ran. His answer is sacrifice. Honorific veneration of exalted figures was common; what no Jew would do was offer sacrificial cult to anyone but YHWH. **No text in the New Testament describes sacrifice offered to Jesus**. On McGrath's criterion, then, nothing the New Testament says about Jesus breaches Jewish monotheism, and the parting of the ways over God's oneness is a second-century affair. Dunn, in *Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?* (2010), came to a convergent verdict from a different direction: the reverence given to Jesus is real and without precedent, and it is nonetheless "of a different order" from the reverence given to God, so that "by and large the first Christians did not worship Jesus as such."

All of this bears directly on Bauckham, whose "divine identity" thesis rests on a binary: a figure is either within the unique identity of God or he is a creature, and the Jewish evidence, Bauckham says, allowed nothing in between. The texts above are the in-between. Daniel Kirk's *A Man Attested by God* (2016) applied that observation to the Synoptic Gospels across six hundred pages and found that "idealized human figure" accounts for every Synoptic datum, the forgiveness of sins, lordship over the Sabbath and the stilling of the storm included, which the early-high-Christology school had been treating as unmistakable divine prerogatives. My own position extends Kirk's reading from the Synoptics to the whole canon by supplying the one thing the Synoptics leave implicit, which is that the human agent is the Word's own enfleshment and not merely its servant.

## 5. The historical argument: what the church believed at the beginning

If belief in the Trinity were essential to being a Christian, we would expect the history of doctrine to show the church holding it from the beginning. What the history shows instead is a church that for three centuries believed something else, and a fourth century in which the something else very nearly won.

R. P. C. Hanson, whose *Search for the Christian Doctrine of God* (1988) remains the fullest study of the fourth-century controversy, concluded that before Nicaea Christian theology was almost universally subordinationist, and Kelly and Grillmeier say much the same. Ayres resists the label as anachronistic, but his own account of the pre-Nicene theologies is of hierarchical schemes in which the Son is ranked after the Father. Justin calls the Logos "another God and Lord, subject to the Maker of all things," distinct from him "in number, not in will" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 56). Irenaeus makes the Son and the Spirit the Father's "two hands." Tertullian, who gave us the word *trinitas*, thinks there was a time when the Son was not yet Son (*Against Hermogenes* 3) and grades the three by degree (*Against Praxeas* 9). Origen calls the Son a *deuteros theos* (*Against Celsus* 5.39) and says the Father exceeds the Son by as much as the Son exceeds the creatures (*Commentary on John* 13.25). Novatian, Hippolytus and Dionysius of Alexandria fall into the same pattern. Hanson's phrase for what happened next is that the fourth century was not a defense of orthodoxy but a search for one, carried on "by the method of trial and error."

Then there is the awkward career of *homoousios* itself. The council that deposed Paul of Samosata at Antioch in 268 rejected the word as a description of the Son's relation to the Father. Athanasius knew this and argued that Paul had used it in a different sense (*On the Synods* 43–45); Hilary conceded the same (*On the Synods* 81, 86); the Homoiousian bishops at Sirmium in 358 cited Antioch 268 against Nicaea outright. Dragoş Andrei Giulea's study of the Antioch material in the *Harvard Theological Review* (2018) finds that the 268 synodals used *ousia* and *hypostasis* interchangeably for the individual, so that their rejection of *homoousios* was an objection to collapsing Father and Son into one subject. Robert Letham's summary of the modern consensus is fair: the word had been used by gnostics for material consubstantiality, "does not seem to have acquired a steady and recognized meaning" by 325, and, as Hanson suggested, was probably chosen because Arius disliked it. I find it hard to see how a term anathematized by a council fifty-seven years before Nicaea, and used at Nicaea in a sense its own champions admitted was not agreed, can be the apostolic deposit.

Nor did Nicaea settle anything. Between 325 and 381 the church produced more than a dozen rival creeds. The "Dated Creed" of Sirmium in 359, and the twin councils of Ariminum and Seleucia the same year, committed the overwhelming majority of the bishops of both East and West to a Homoian formula that dropped *ousia*-language altogether; Jerome's remark that the whole world groaned and marvelled to find itself Arian is a description of the result. The Nicene party's victory in 381 owed a great deal to Theodosius' edict *Cunctos populos* of the year before, which made the faith of Damasus and Peter of Alexandria the legal definition of Christianity before the Council of Constantinople had met. Even then the creed of 325 had said only "and in the Holy Spirit"; the Spirit's divinity was asserted in 381 and the word *homoousios* was never applied to the Spirit in any ecumenical creed. The Athanasian Creed, which supplies the "whoever wishes to be saved" clause, is a Latin composition of the late fifth or sixth century with no connection to Athanasius.

It is easy to claim too much from this history, so I should be precise. The ante-Nicene writers were two-subject subordinationists, not agency Christologists in my sense. Justin’s Logos is a second God, numerically distinct from the Father, and Origen’s is a deuteros theos with a hypostasis of his own; the positive Christology of §2 can take no comfort from them. What the history establishes, and establishes completely, is the sufficiency thesis. The Nicene who holds that Trinitarian belief is necessary must say either that Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen and the bishops of Ariminum were not Christians, or that what it takes to be a Christian changed in the fourth century. The tradition will not say the first of men it venerates as fathers and saints. The second concedes the point: whatever made Justin a Christian in 155 did not include the doctrine of 381, and the New Testament has not changed since. Whether the Word is a second subject or God’s own act is a separate question, taken up in §3 and §4.

## 6. The systematic argument: a repair that needed repairing

I have come to think of the Trinity less as a discovery than as a patch. Once the church had committed itself to saying that the Son is God in the same sense that the Father is God, three difficulties appeared which the New Testament had never had, and each of them needed a further doctrine to keep it contained.

The first difficulty is the Jesus of the Gospels. He does not know the day or the hour (Mark 13:32). He is tempted (Heb 4:15), although God cannot be (James 1:13). He grows in wisdom (Luke 2:52). He asks why anyone should call him good when no one is good but God (Mark 10:18). He prays that the cup may pass and submits his will to another's (Mark 14:36). He cries that God has forsaken him, and he dies. None of this troubles an agency reading, in which a human agent of God is ignorant of some things, tempted, obedient and mortal because he is human. For the Nicene reading each item is a contradiction, and the solution devised for them was Chalcedon's formula of 451: one person, two natures, each keeping its own properties. The formula is not in Scripture. It split the church at the moment it was promulgated, and the Oriental Orthodox communion, Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Malankara, remains out of communion with the Chalcedonian churches over it to this day. Chalcedon exists because Nicaea created a problem. An Agency Christology never creates it.

The second difficulty is the word "person." The doctrine speaks of three *hypostaseis* in one *ousia*, and Augustine confessed that the Latin *persona* was used in the formula "not in order to say that, but in order not to be silent" (*De Trinitate* 5.9). The recent analytic literature has made his embarrassment precise. Social Trinitarians such as Swinburne, Cornelius Plantinga Jr. and Hasker take the persons to be three centres of consciousness and will, and are told by their Latin colleagues that this is tritheism; Alston, reviewing Swinburne, observed that anyone who goes beyond reciting the creedal phrases "inevitably leans" toward either modalism or tritheism. Latin Trinitarians such as Leftow and Brower and Rea make the persons something less than distinct subjects and are told by the social camp that this is modalism. The relative-identity theorists, Geach and van Inwagen among them, keep both halves of the doctrine by denying that identity is absolute, which most philosophers regard as too high a price. Each school holds that the other two are outside orthodoxy. The principal papers on all three sides, Leftow’s critique of social Trinitarianism among them, are collected in Thomas McCall and Michael Rea’s *Philosophical and Theological Essays on the Trinity* (2009). Beau Branson's 2019 taxonomy, "No New Solutions to the Logical Problem of the Trinity," argues that every possible solution falls into a small, exhaustive set of types, each of them controversial, heretical or inconsistent, so that no importantly new solution remains to be found. Branson is himself a Trinitarian who looks to the monarchy of the Father for a way through, but his map of the options is the one the debate now works with, and Dale Tuggy's Stanford Encyclopedia entry, maintained since 2009, sets out the same rival families and the objections each faces. I do not see how a doctrine whose most able defenders cannot agree on what it asserts, and who each take the others' version to be heresy, can be the thing one must believe in order to be saved.

The third difficulty is eternal generation. For the Son to be uncreated and yet derived from the Father, Nicaea needed him to be "begotten, not made," and begotten eternally. The texts offered in support were *monogenēs*, which I discussed above; Psalm 2:7, which the New Testament applies to the resurrection (Acts 13:33) and the exaltation (Heb 1:5; 5:5); and Proverbs 8:22–25, where Wisdom is brought forth before the hills. But the Septuagint of Proverbs 8:22 reads *ektisen*, "he created," which is why it was Arius' favorite verse and why Athanasius devoted most of the second *Discourse against the Arians* to explaining it away. Eternal generation is something the system requires, not something revelation supplies. On an agency reading God's Word is uncreated because it is God's own, and nothing has to be begotten to secure the point.

There is a cleaner way to put the whole of this, which is to ask what each theory would lead us to expect in the texts before we open them, and then to look. Hurtado and Bauckham rest their case on four features: the application of YHWH-texts to Jesus, creation "through" him, the bestowal of the divine name, and early cultic practice. All four are real and exactly what an agency Christology predicts, since an agent bearing God's name is addressed by God's texts, acts as God's instrument in creation, and receives the honor of the one who sent him. Those features are therefore common ground, and a theory that offers them as proof of divine identity has mistaken the shared evidence for its own. What divides the theories is the second group of features, where the two make opposite predictions.

| Feature of the text | Divine identity predicts | Agency predicts | Does it discriminate? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| YHWH-texts applied to Jesus (Phil 2:10 ← Isa 45:23; Rom 10:13 ← Joel 2:32) | Yes | Yes: the agent bears the name (Exod 23:21) | No |
| Creation "through" Jesus (1 Cor 8:6; Col 1:16; Heb 1:2) | Yes | Yes: *dia* is the agent's preposition | No |
| A creation psalm addressed to the Son as maker, not instrument (Heb 1:10–12 ← Ps 102:25–27) | Yes | Only because the Son is the enfleshment of God's own creative Word (Ps 33:6) | **Partly: divine identity** |
| The name above every name given to him (Phil 2:9) | Yes | Yes: given, not possessed | No |
| Early cult: prayer, hymns, baptism in his name, *maranatha* | Yes | Yes: honour of the sent one is honour of the sender | No |
| Lordship conferred at exaltation (Acts 2:36; Phil 2:9; Matt 28:18) | No: lordship is intrinsic and eternal | Yes | **Yes: agency** |
| Worship terminating on the Father (Phil 2:11; Rom 16:27; Eph 3:21) | No: co-equal persons receive co-equal glory | Yes | **Yes: agency** |
| The exalted Christ still has a God (John 20:17; Rev 3:2, 12; Eph 1:17) | No | Yes | **Yes: agency** |
| The Son finally subjected to God (1 Cor 15:28) | No | Yes | **Yes: agency** |
| Sacrificial cult to Jesus | Expected if Jesus is YHWH | Absent | **Yes: agency** (absent) |
| Spirit addressed in prayer or doxology, or named as a source of grace in a Pauline greeting | Expected of a third person | Not expected | **Yes: agency** (absent) |
| Jesus' ignorance, temptation, and submission (Mark 13:32; 14:36; Heb 4:15) | Requires partition by nature | Ordinary | **Yes: agency** |

Four features are shared, seven discriminate in favor of agency, and one, Hebrews 1:10, leans the other way. Even that one an agency account can absorb only because it makes the Word God's own creative act rather than God's servant; an account on which Jesus is merely an exalted man could not absorb it at all. The reply I usually hear at this point is that the New Testament is "not yet" Nicene but contains the materials from which Nicaea was built. I take that to be a concession: a theory whose predictions are satisfied only by a later development is a theory about the later development.

A fair critic will notice an asymmetry in how I have read the evidence. I take the "low" texts, the conferred lordship and the "my God" sayings and 1 Corinthians 15:28, at face value, and I read the "high" texts, John 1:1 and 20:28 and Colossians 1, within a category that keeps them short of identity. The Nicene does the reverse, taking the high texts at face value and partitioning the low ones by nature. The asymmetry is not arbitrary on my side. Agency is a category the first century already possessed, and it accounts for both bodies of evidence with a single apparatus: the agent is honored as the sender and remains subordinate to him, so the high texts and the low texts are the two faces of one relation. The Nicene category accounts for the high texts and then needs a second doctrine, two natures in one person, to absorb the low ones. Given two readings each of which must subordinate one set of texts to the other, the one that does so with a native category and no auxiliary machinery is the better reading, and that is the choice I am making. The same calculus holds across the board: the Nicene account needs eternal generation, two natures, the communication of idioms, the self-effacement of the Spirit and a non-standard logic of identity, each brought in to manage a difficulty the move before it had produced, while the agency account needs the single claim that God's self-expression became a human life which God then raised to lordship. On parsimony, at least, the agency account has the advantage.

## 7. Replies to Nicene objections

**7.1 "The earliest Christology was already the highest" (Hurtado, Bauckham)**

Larry Hurtado’s *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) and Richard Bauckham’s *God Crucified* (1998) established, I think beyond reasonable dispute, that cultic devotion to Jesus is as early as our earliest sources: prayer through him and to him, hymns about him, baptism in his name, the *maranatha* invocation. I grant every bit of it. The disagreement is over what this devotion means. Hurtado’s own explanation is that it was a “mutation” within Jewish monotheism driven by religious experience, and he is careful not to claim that the first Christians held the Son to be of one essence with the Father.

Bauckham claims more: that Jesus is “included in the unique divine identity.” That claim has drawn sustained criticism. Tuggy showed in “On Bauckham’s Bargain” (*Theology Today*, 2013) that “identity” must mean either numerical identity, in which case the thesis is self-contradictory (Jesus would be the Father), or a looser sense of “identifying features,” in which case it is too thin to deliver the Nicene conclusion Bauckham wants from it. The evidence in §4 tells against Bauckham’s claim that Second Temple Judaism offered no real precedent for a figure who shares God’s throne and receives worship. And Bauckham’s own framework concedes the decisive point, since he classes Word, Wisdom and Spirit as personifications included in God’s identity without being distinct hypostases. If the Word can be within the divine identity without being a second person, so can the Word made flesh.

The early-high-Christology school has shown that Jesus was worshipped early. It has not shown that the worshippers were proto-Nicenes, and Dunn, McGrath, Kirk, Casey and Ehrman have each argued at length that they were not. The case has since been pressed further by Simon Gathercole on pre-existence in the Synoptics (*The Preexistent Son*, 2006), Chris Tilling on the relational shape of Paul’s Christology (*Paul’s Divine Christology*, 2012), and Crispin Fletcher-Louis on the boundaries of Jewish monotheism (*Jesus Monotheism*, 2015). Each deserves a fuller reply than this essay can give, and I hope to offer one separately.

**7.2 "Worship of anyone but God is idolatry"**

The premise is simply untrue of Second Temple Judaism and of the New Testament. The Enochic Son of Man is worshipped (1 Enoch 48:5; 62:9); the angels are commanded to worship Adam; Moses is venerated on God's throne in the *Exagoge*. Inside the New Testament the saints will receive the obeisance of the synagogue of Satan (Rev 3:9), and the Lamb is worshipped *because* he was slain and has been given the scroll (Rev 5:9–12), which is to say because of what God has done for him. The text that settles the matter for me is Philippians 2:9–11. Every knee bows to Jesus because God "highly exalted him and gave him the name," and the bowing is "to the glory of God the Father." The worship is derivative, conferred, and directed through Jesus to the Father, which is the *shaliach* pattern exactly. Isaiah 42:8, "my glory I will not give to another," forbids sharing glory with a rival god; it says nothing against honouring the agent in whom God has placed his own name (Exod 23:21). McGrath's point sharpens all this: the real boundary of Jewish monotheism was sacrificial cult, and no one in the New Testament ever offers a sacrifice to Jesus.

**7.3 The classical proof-texts**

| Text | Nicene reading | Agent reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| John 1:1 | The Word is a divine person distinct from and equal to the Father | The Word is God's own self-expression (*theos* as predicate, not identity); 1:18 (reading *monogenēs theos*) defines its role as exegesis of the Father |
| John 5:18–26 | Jesus accepts the charge of equality; "life in himself" given to the Son is eternal generation | 5:19–23 is the agency principle verbatim: the Son does only what he sees the Father do, is honoured "just as" the Father *because* judgment was given him and the Father sent him. 5:26 says the Father *gave* the Son life in himself; a gift is the agency pattern, not an eternal relation |
| John 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am") | Claim to the divine name | The claim is to the Word’s pre-existence, which the Prologue has already asserted, and the “I am” is the agent’s prerogative: at the bush it is “the angel of the LORD” who appears (Exod 3:2), and the voice from the bush says “I am who I am” (3:14). The attempted stoning (8:59) follows a claim to be greater than Abraham (8:53–57) |
| John 10:30 ("I and the Father are one") | Unity of essence | *Hen* is neuter; the same word describes the unity of believers with one another and with God in 17:21–22. Unity of will and action, as Jesus explains in 10:37–38 |
| John 17:5 ("the glory I had with you before the world was") | A pre-existent person recalls shared glory | The Word had glory with God before the world and now speaks as a man. The same prayer calls this glory "given" (17:22) and grounds "before the foundation of the world" in the Father's love (17:24) |
| John 20:28 ("my Lord and my God") | Confession of deity | Thomas recognises God present in Jesus (14:9); eleven verses earlier the same Jesus has said "my God" (20:17). On the agency principle, to address the agent as the sender is correct. A psalmist addresses a human king directly as *elohim* (Ps 45:6), the text Heb 1:8 applies to the Son, and John's own Jesus defends his title from Ps 82, "I said, you are gods" (10:34–36). Three verses later the evangelist states his purpose: that readers believe Jesus is "the Messiah, the Son of God" (20:31), not that he is God |
| Rom 9:5 | "Christ, who is God over all" | Punctuation is editorial; the doxology "God who is over all be blessed forever" is Paul's standard form (2 Cor 11:31). Paul never elsewhere calls Christ *ho theos* |
| Titus 2:13; 2 Pet 1:1 | Granville Sharp: "our great God and Saviour, Jesus Christ" | Sharp's rule is contested for proper names and titles; and even granted, "God" applied to the exalted agent is licensed by Ps 45:6 and Exod 7:1 ("I have made you God to Pharaoh") |
| Heb 1:8–12 | Son addressed as God and as the unchanging Creator of Ps 102 | 1:8 quotes Ps 45, addressed to a human king called *elohim*; the next verse says "God, *your God*, has anointed you." The psalm itself holds "God" and "has a God" of one subject with no two-natures apparatus. 1:10–12 applies Ps 102's creation language to the Son, and on this account that is exactly right: the heavens were made "by the word of the LORD" (Ps 33:6), and the Word is God's own creative act, so a creation psalm addressed to the Word made flesh is addressed to God acting. The permanence clauses (1:11–12) say of the Son what Isa 40:8 says of God's Word: it stands forever. What the passage does not say is that a second divine subject did the creating |
| Col 1:15–20; Heb 1:2–3 | Creator | Creation *through* (*dia*) and *in* the Son, never *from* (*ek*) him; the Son is "firstborn of all creation" and "heir." Col 1:16 also says all things are "for" (*eis*) him, the phrase 1 Cor 8:6 uses of the Father, but that is the heir's claim on an estate he has been appointed to receive (Heb 1:2). This is Wisdom's role (Prov 8; Wis 7:22; Philo), transferred to the Word that became flesh |
| Phil 2:6–11 | Pre-existent divine person possessing equality with God | The single subject is the Word, narrated the way Sirach 24 narrates Wisdom, who receives her Creator's command, obeys it, and descends to pitch her tent in Jacob (24:8; cf. John 1:14), and who is then identified with something concrete in Israel's history, the book of the covenant (24:23). Choice and obedience are given to a personification without making it a second hypostasis, and one subject spans pre-existence and embodiment. So here: in God's form, the Word did not exploit what was its own, emptied itself in becoming human, and, as Jesus, was obedient to death and exalted. What it did not grasp at was co-ordinate status. The hymn ends with God exalting Jesus and the glory going to the Father |

**I am not claiming that the Nicene reading of each of these verses is impossible. I am claiming that no verse *requires* three co-equal persons**, and that the cumulative pattern of agency verbs, asymmetric prepositions, Jesus' God, and conferred lordship points steadily the other way.

**7.4 "God is love, so there must be eternal relations within God"**

This argument goes back to Richard of St Victor and has been revived by Swinburne, and it proves a good deal too much. If God needs an eternal other in order to be love, then by parity of reasoning he needs an eternal creation in order to be creator, an eternal recipient in order to be merciful, and an eternal wrongdoer in order to be just, all of which the Nicene tradition denies. The New Testament itself grounds "God is love" in an act rather than a structure: "in this the love of God was made manifest among us, that God sent his only Son into the world" (1 John 4:9). An agency Christology locates God's love exactly where John does.

**7.5 "Only God can save"**

I agree, and so does the New Testament, which says that God did: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Cor 5:19); "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8). The objection assumes that for God to be the one acting, a second divine person must do the acting. But God acting through his own enfleshed Word is God acting. What the New Testament actually insists on, when it talks about the mediator, is his humanity: "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5); "since by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection" (1 Cor 15:21); "he had to be made like his brothers in every respect" (Heb 2:17). Gregory's slogan, that what is not assumed is not healed, cuts toward a fully human agent, not away from one.

**7.6 "Ignorance is innocent; rejection of defined doctrine is not" (Newman)**

Newman's *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* (1845) holds that later definitions make explicit what was implicit from the beginning, and the argument is often brought in at this point to say that while ignorance of the Trinity was innocent in the second century, rejection of it after 381 is something else. I do not think the theory rescues the necessity claim. If the doctrine was implicit in the apostolic faith, then holding the apostolic faith was sufficient before 325, and nothing in that faith changed afterwards; what changed was the church's description of it, and declining a description is not the same as rejecting the thing described. Newman's theory also needs an authority competent to make descriptions binding, and that is precisely what Protestants deny to councils and what the fourth-century councils demonstrably lacked, since Ariminum outvoted Nicaea. And Newman's own tests of a true development, preservation of type and continuity of principle, are failed by a development that reverses a prior conciliar judgment, as Nicaea reversed Antioch 268 on *homoousios*.

**7.7 "The councils carry the Spirit's authority"**

As a defence of the Trinity this is circular: the councils that defined the Trinity are authoritative because the Spirit guided them, and we know the Spirit guided them because of what they defined. But the record is also, on its face, a record of reversals. Antioch 268 condemned *homoousios*; Nicaea in 325 adopted it; Sirmium in 357 banned *ousia*-language altogether; Ariminum and Seleucia in 359 committed the great majority of bishops to the Homoian creed; Constantinople in 381 restored Nicaea and extended it to the Spirit; Ephesus in 431 condemned Nestorius on terms Chalcedon in 451 partly rehabilitated; Chalcedon split the church; Constantinople in 553 condemned the Three Chapters that Chalcedon had accepted. Rome and the East then divided permanently over the *filioque*, which is a Trinitarian dispute and is still unresolved. If this is the Spirit's guidance, it is guidance toward a faith that no single communion holds in its entirety.

**7.8 "Agent Christology collapses into modalism or into two Sons"**

The dilemma helps itself to the thing in dispute, which is whether "the Word" names a person at all. A modalist says there is one person who wears three masks. I say there are two subjects in the Gospel story, God and the man Jesus, and that Jesus prays to, obeys, is sent by, and has a God, none of which a modalist can say. The "two Sons" charge, which was the accusation thrown at Nestorius, needs a pre-existent divine Son to be one of the two, and I posit no such Son. There is God's Word, which is God acting, and there is Jesus, in whom that acting is a human life. The union between them is not a partnership of two persons; it is the identity of one human life with God's self-expression. I think this is what Chalcedon's "without confusion, without division" was reaching for, and that the council overshot by exactly one hypostasis. When a Nicene presses the dilemma on me, he is asking me to answer a question that only arises once his premises are granted, and the right response is to decline the premises.

**7.9 "The parting of the ways proves Jews saw Christ-devotion as a breach of monotheism"**

McGrath's redating of the parting over monotheism to the second century, and Boyarin's work on how late, and how politically motivated, the rabbinic construction of the "two powers" heresy was, together suggest that the Jewish quarrel with the church was first messianic and halakhic and only later theological. Paul was persecuted for Torah-free Gentile inclusion, not for binitarian worship. As for John 5:18, where Jesus is said to be "making himself equal with God," that is the accusation of his opponents, and Jesus' reply in the verses that follow is the fullest statement of the agency principle anywhere in the Gospel: the Son does nothing of himself, judges as he hears, and seeks the will of the one who sent him.

## 8. Conclusion

**What makes someone a Christian, on the evidence I have set out, is trust in the one God, the Father, and in Jesus whom he sent, raised and made Lord.** That is the confession of Romans 10:9 and 1 Corinthians 8:6 and John 17:3, and of every baptism Luke records. It is the faith of Justin and Irenaeus and Tertullian and Origen, and it is the faith the bishops signed at Ariminum in 359 and the Homoian missionaries carried to the Goths. Nicene Trinitarianism is a theory about that confession, cast in a borrowed vocabulary, enforced by imperial edict, and revised at every council that touched it afterwards. Theories about the faith may be good or bad. They are not the faith, and I do not believe that sixteen centuries of repeating one turns it into the faith. The same is true of the theory I have offered here. **Agent Christology is not the faith either; it is an attempt to say what the faith confesses in the categories its first confessors used, and nothing in it is a condition of anyone’s salvation. It will not persuade everyone, and it need not. But I hope it helps some who are wrestling with God.**

Three objections tend to come up at the end of an argument like this. Rather than wait for them, I’ll address them here.

1. It will be said that God's act must after all be a subject, because it prays and obeys. But the one who prays and obeys in the narratives is Jesus of Nazareth, who is a human being. The Nicene reads a second divine subject behind him and is then obliged to explain why that subject is ignorant, tempted and forsaken. I have no second subject to explain. The pre-incarnate language of John 1 and Philippians 2 is the personifying idiom of Proverbs 8, Wisdom 7 and Philo, where God's Word "comes" and "dwells" and "is sent" without anyone in that literature supposing a second hypostasis. It falls to the Nicene to show that John meant by *logos* what no Jewish writer before him had meant, and that has not been done.
2. It will be said that no Jewish agent ever received universal worship or was called "the first and the last." The agency texts give us enthroned, named and venerated figures; the New Testament gives us one such figure raised from the dead and seated at God's right hand, and his honors exceed theirs because his vindication did. As for the title, it is spoken in Revelation by one "who was dead and came to life" (2:8), which YHWH never was, and it is borne the way the name is borne in Exodus 23:21, by the one in whom God has placed it. Bauckham's strongest text turns out to be the clearest instance of the agency principle.
3. It will be said, more subtly, that the Trinity marks the church's identity even if it is not a condition of anyone's salvation. I think that concession gives up most of what is in dispute. A boundary that the church of the first three centuries could not have crossed is a boundary drawn around something other than apostolic Christianity, and the New Testament knows no class of the saved who are not Christians. A definition that creates such a class has defined the wrong thing.

The reply that remains to the Nicene tradition is that the church, guided by the Spirit, has settled the matter, and that private exegesis has no standing to reopen it. That reply is available only to someone who already grants the councils the authority the argument was supposed to establish, and it was not available to the councils themselves, which contradicted one another for two generations until an emperor chose a side. For the rest of us the question is what the apostles taught and what the first Christians believed, and on that question I do not think the evidence is balanced. One God, the Father; one Lord, Jesus Christ, his agent and his Word made flesh; the Spirit of them both. That was enough to be a Christian in the year 60. I see no reason it is not enough now.
